The pink-and-gold sunset sky seen from the hilltop: the very large plum-purple dragon coming down over the mountain ridge with wings spread wide, small silver smoke rings puffing from his nose; behind him the small rose-pink dragon landing clumsily and joyfully in the grass, wings every which way; Nell laughing, and Bo with both arms straight up shouting hello; the empty iron beacon on its tall pole at the edge of frame. Warm rose and gold light, floating sparkles. Gentle dragons — mouths closed, no teeth showing, big soft round eyes, nothing frightening. No other people, no other children, no other animals. Plum: a very large grown dragon, three or four times Pip's size — deep plum-purple all over with translucent lavender-violet wing membranes, a long neck and a long tail with a soft violet fin running along it, four gently curving lilac horns, calm dark-violet eyes under heavy kind lids, big rounded feet with blunt pale claws, a broad flat nose that puffs small silver smoke rings; unhurried and gentle, like a hillside that decided to be a dragon. Pip: a small young dragon, about the size of a big dog — warm rose-pink all over, smooth pink scales with a paler blush-pink belly, soft pearly-pink wings with a faint shimmer, a short rounded snout, two tiny blunt lilac horns, big round amber-gold eyes with long lashes, a stubby tail with a small pink fin at the tip; blunt little teeth that never show; one faintly singed patch along a wing edge; clearly a young dragon, never a person, never a child. Nell: a seven-year-old girl, warm brown skin, thick dark curls tied back with a strip of yellow cloth, large dark eyes, a gap in her front teeth when she grins; a patched grey-green wool coat too big at the sleeves, muddy boots; a small tin whistle on a cord around her neck. Bo: a four-year-old boy, Nell's little brother — the same warm brown skin and dark curls, round cheeks, huge serious dark eyes; a bright red knitted woolen hat with a floppy pom-pom that he never takes off; a mustard-yellow scarf wound twice around his neck; he carries one stick of kindling like it is an important job.
